Planning a cloud migration that doesn't take down your business

Migrations go wrong in predictable ways. Here is the sequence we use, and the specific decisions that keep a cutover from turning into a lost Monday.

Technauf engineering·9 min read

Migrations fail in the planning, not the execution

Almost every migration horror story traces back to something nobody mapped: an application with a hard-coded server name, a licence tied to a MAC address, a nightly job on a machine no one remembered owning.

The execution weekend is the easy part if the discovery was honest.

Step 1 — Inventory everything, including what nobody admits to

Automated discovery gets you maybe 80% of the picture. The remaining 20% comes from sitting with each department and asking what they use, especially the spreadsheets and small tools that never went through IT.

  • Every server, its role, and who owns it
  • Every application and its authentication method
  • Scheduled jobs, integrations, and anything with an API key
  • Licence terms — some are not portable to cloud infrastructure

Step 2 — Map dependencies before you decide sequence

You cannot move an application before the database it talks to, and you cannot move either before you understand what else connects to them. We map dependencies as an actual diagram, then derive the move sequence from it.

If a provider gives you a migration date before they have given you a dependency map, they are guessing.

Step 3 — Pick the migration pattern per workload

Not everything should move the same way. For each workload the honest choice is usually one of four:

  1. Rehost — lift and shift as-is, fastest, least benefit
  2. Replatform — minor changes to use managed services
  3. Refactor — rebuild to be cloud-native, slowest, most benefit
  4. Retire — a surprising amount of what you inventory is genuinely unused

The retire category is the one clients underestimate. In most migrations we find 10–20% of workloads that nobody has used in a year.

Step 4 — Pilot with a real group of real people

A pilot with the IT team proves nothing, because the IT team knows the workarounds. Pick a department that uses the system hard, move them first, and let them break it while rollback is still cheap.

Step 5 — Write the cutover runbook, hour by hour

The runbook lists every step, who does it, how long it takes, and how to verify it worked. It also contains the point of no return — the moment after which rolling back costs more than pushing forward — stated explicitly, with a named person who makes that call.

Step 6 — The rollback plan most providers skip

Ask any prospective provider what happens if the cutover fails at 3am Sunday. If the answer is vague, that is your answer about the whole engagement.

A real rollback plan means the old environment stays intact and functional until the new one has been validated under load — not decommissioned Friday to save a month of hosting.

Step 7 — Validate under real load, then decommission

Run both environments in parallel for a defined period. Validate performance under Monday-morning load, not weekend quiet. Only then decommission, and only after a final backup of the old environment is confirmed restorable.

What a realistic timeline looks like

For a 50-person business with a typical mix of file servers, a line-of-business application, and email: two weeks discovery, two weeks design and pilot, one cutover weekend, and two weeks parallel run before decommissioning. Anyone promising materially faster is skipping one of those.
