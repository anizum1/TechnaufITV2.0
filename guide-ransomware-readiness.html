Ransomware readiness for small businesses: the 9 controls that actually matter

Most ransomware guidance is written for enterprises with a security team. If you have 20–200 staff and no dedicated security hire, here is the list that actually moves your risk — in the order we deploy it.

Technauf engineering·11 min read

Why the order matters

Every ransomware checklist you find online is technically correct and practically useless, because it lists forty controls with no sense of which ones stop an actual attack. In the incidents we have worked, the same small handful of gaps show up again and again.

The nine below are ordered by how much risk they remove per dollar and per hour of effort. If you only get through the first four, you have still closed the majority of the paths we see used in real attacks.

1. Multi-factor authentication on every account — including admins

This is the single highest-value control, and it is the one most often half-implemented. We routinely find businesses with MFA on staff mailboxes but not on the administrator accounts that could reconfigure the whole tenant.

Service accounts and break-glass admin accounts are the ones attackers look for precisely because they are so often exempted. If an account cannot use standard MFA, it needs compensating controls — not an exemption and a shrug.

2. Backups that are immutable and actually tested

Modern ransomware deletes or encrypts backups first. A backup that the same compromised admin credentials can delete is not a backup — it is a copy.

What good looks like:

  • Off-site copies with immutable retention that cannot be deleted within the retention window
  • Restores tested quarterly with a written result, not assumed to work
  • At least one copy outside the identity system that a domain compromise would reach

3. Endpoint detection and response, not legacy antivirus

Signature-based antivirus catches known malware. Ransomware operators use living-off-the-land techniques and freshly compiled payloads specifically to defeat it. EDR watches behaviour instead — mass file encryption looks abnormal regardless of what binary is doing it.

The capability that matters most in practice is automatic isolation: when a device starts behaving like it is encrypting a share, it gets pulled off the network within seconds, not after someone reads an alert email.

4. Patch the things that face the internet, fast

You do not need perfect patch coverage everywhere. You need ruthless patching of anything internet-facing: VPN appliances, firewalls, remote access gateways, and public web applications.

  1. Inventory everything with a public IP or an inbound port
  2. Set a hard SLA — critical patches on internet-facing systems within 72 hours
  3. Track exceptions in writing, with a compensating control and a review date

5. Remove standing administrative rights

If everyday user accounts have local administrator rights, a single successful phish gives the attacker everything that account can reach. Removing standing admin rights is disruptive for about two weeks and then nobody notices.

6. Segment the network so one machine is not the whole company

Flat networks are why a single infected laptop in reception becomes a company-wide outage. Warehouse scanners, guest Wi-Fi, CCTV, and finance workstations have no business being able to reach each other directly.

7. Turn on and centralise logging before you need it

After an incident, the first question your insurer and your lawyers will ask is what the attacker accessed. Without centralised logs retained beyond the attacker's dwell time, the honest answer is that you cannot tell — and you will likely have to notify as though everything was taken.

8. Train people on the attacks they will actually see

Annual hour-long compliance training changes nothing. Short, frequent, realistic phishing simulations do, because they build the reflex to hesitate on the specific kinds of messages your staff actually receive.

The metric worth tracking is not the click rate alone but the report rate — how many people flag the simulation. A workforce that reports quickly gives you the minutes that matter.

9. Write the incident response plan while it is calm

At 2am during a live incident, nobody is going to invent a good decision-making process. The plan does not need to be long — it needs to name who decides, who calls the insurer, who talks to staff, and where the offline copy of the plan lives.

Keep a printed copy. We have worked incidents where the response plan itself was on the encrypted file share.

Where most businesses actually are

In our assessments across DFW, the typical 50-person business has items 1, 3, and 8 partially in place and nothing else. Closing items 2, 4, and 5 usually takes a few weeks and removes far more risk than any new product purchase.
