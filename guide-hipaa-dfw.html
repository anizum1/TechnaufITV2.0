HIPAA for DFW medical practices: what your IT provider is responsible for

HIPAA splits responsibility between your practice and your IT provider in ways that are rarely spelled out. Here is where the line actually falls, and what evidence you need on hand.

Technauf engineering·12 min read

The Business Associate Agreement is the starting point, not the finish line

Any IT provider with access to systems holding protected health information is a business associate and must sign a BAA. That agreement makes them directly liable for safeguarding that data.

A signed BAA does not mean your provider is doing the technical work. We have reviewed practices with a perfectly valid BAA and no encryption, no access logging, and no tested backups. The paperwork was in order and the safeguards were not.

The technical safeguards your provider should own

The HIPAA Security Rule's technical safeguards map cleanly onto IT work. In practice your provider should own all five:

  • Access control — unique user IDs, automatic logoff, encryption and decryption
  • Audit controls — logging and review of access to systems holding PHI
  • Integrity controls — protection against improper alteration or destruction
  • Authentication — verifying that a person is who they claim to be
  • Transmission security — encryption of PHI in transit

What auditors actually ask to see

Auditors rarely ask whether you have a control. They ask you to evidence it. The difference matters enormously.

  1. A current risk analysis, dated within the last year, covering all systems touching PHI
  2. Documentation of each identified risk and what was done about it
  3. Access logs showing who accessed PHI and when, retained for six years
  4. Evidence that backups have been restored successfully, not just taken
  5. Records of workforce security training with dates and attendees

The gaps we most often find in North Texas practices

Across assessments for DFW medical practices, the same five findings recur:

  • Departed staff whose accounts were disabled but never removed from the EHR
  • Backups running but never restore-tested, sometimes for years
  • Audit logging enabled but nobody reviewing it, which the rule requires
  • Personal devices accessing email with PHI and no mobile device management
  • The risk analysis done once at some point in the past and never updated

Encryption is technically addressable — and practically mandatory

HIPAA labels encryption as 'addressable' rather than 'required', which some providers misread as optional. Addressable means you must implement it or document a legitimate reason why an equivalent alternative is appropriate.

In 2026 there is no defensible reason not to encrypt laptops, mobile devices, and backups. The breach notification safe harbour for properly encrypted data also makes it the single most cost-effective control you can implement.

The six-year retention rule catches people out

HIPAA requires documentation to be retained for six years from creation or last effective date. That includes policies, risk analyses, training records, and access logs. A logging system that rotates after 90 days does not meet this, and it is one of the most common infrastructure gaps we correct.

What to ask your current provider this week

Three questions will tell you most of what you need to know:

  1. When was our last restore test, and can I see the written result?
  2. Can you show me who accessed patient records last Tuesday?
  3. When was our risk analysis last updated, and what changed since?

If any answer takes more than a day to produce, that is the gap worth closing first.
