Backups that survive ransomware: a practical strategy

Nearly every business we assess says it has backups. Far fewer can say when they last restored from one. That gap is where ransomware recoveries go wrong.

Technauf engineering·8 min read

"We have backups" is not the claim that matters

The claim that matters is: we can restore this specific system, to this point in time, within this many hours, and we have proven it recently. Almost nobody can make that claim without preparation — and the middle of an incident is a poor time to find out.

Backup jobs that report success routinely turn out to have been silently skipping the database, or backing up to a share the attacker also encrypted.

3-2-1, and the two extra digits worth adding

The classic rule is three copies of your data, on two different media types, with one copy off-site. Ransomware pushed the industry to extend it:

  • 3 copies of the data
  • 2 different media or platforms
  • 1 copy off-site
  • 1 copy immutable or offline — cannot be altered or deleted within its retention window, even with valid admin credentials
  • 0 errors on the last verified restore test

The final two are what turn a backup strategy into a ransomware strategy. Modern attackers hunt for backup infrastructure first and delete what they find, using the same administrator credentials your backup software uses.

Immutability is the control that actually stops the attack

If your backups live on a share reachable with domain credentials, they are not a separate line of defence — they are part of the same environment being attacked. Immutable storage enforces retention at the storage layer, so a delete request is refused regardless of who makes it.

Ask your provider a direct question: if an attacker had our domain administrator password right now, could they delete our backups? If the answer is anything other than a confident no, that is the gap.

Set recovery objectives before choosing technology

Two numbers drive every backup decision, and both are business decisions rather than technical ones:

  • RPO — how much data you can afford to lose, which sets backup frequency
  • RTO — how long you can afford to be down, which sets recovery method

A business that can lose a day of data and be down for two days needs something very different from a medical practice that can afford neither. Deciding these first prevents both overspending and nasty surprises.

What a real restore test looks like

Confirming that a backup job reported success is monitoring, not testing. A restore test means actually recovering something and verifying it works:

  1. Pick a real system — rotate through them across the year, prioritising the ones the business cannot operate without
  2. Restore it to an isolated environment, not over the top of production
  3. Have someone who uses that system daily confirm the data is complete and current
  4. Record how long the whole thing took, start to finish
  5. Write down what went wrong, because something always does the first time

That recorded duration is your real RTO. It is almost always longer than anyone assumed, and knowing the true number is the entire point of the exercise.

The gaps we find most often

  • Backups running to a network share that is reachable — and therefore encryptable — from any infected workstation
  • Cloud platforms such as Microsoft 365 assumed to be backed up by the provider; retention policies are not backups
  • The backup job succeeding for years while silently excluding a database added after it was configured
  • No off-site copy, because the off-site step failed quietly eighteen months ago
  • Documentation for restoring stored on the file server that would need restoring

A reasonable place to start

If you do nothing else this quarter: confirm one copy is immutable, restore one important system end to end, and write down how long it took. Those three actions will tell you more about your actual resilience than any amount of dashboard-watching.
